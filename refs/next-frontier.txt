Large Language Model (LLM) applications, including LLM app stores and autonomous agents, are shaping
the future of AI ecosystems. However, platform silos, fragmented hardware integration, and the absence
of standardized interfaces limit scalability, interoperability, and resource efficiency. While LLM app stores
democratize AI, their closed ecosystems restrict modular AI reuse and cross-platform portability. Meanwhile,
agent-based frameworks offer flexibility but often lack seamless integration across diverse environments.
This paper envisions the future of LLM applications and proposes a three-layer decoupled architecture
grounded in software engineering principles such as layered system design, service-oriented architectures,
and hardware-software co-design. This architecture separates application logic, communication protocols, and
hardware execution, enhancing modularity, efficiency, and cross-platform compatibility. Beyond architecture,
we highlight key security and privacy challenges for safe, scalable AI deployment and outline research
directions in software and security engineering. This vision aims to foster open, secure, and interoperable
LLM ecosystems, guiding future advancements in AI applications.
1 INTRODUCTION
Large Language Models (LLMs) have significantly advanced artificial intelligence by enabling a
broad spectrum of applications, ranging from content generation and conversational agents to auto-
mated reasoning and decision support systems [12, 39]. These applications have primarily evolved
through two paradigms:LLM apps hosted within centralized LLM app stores, such as OpenAI’s
GPT Store[25], Quora’s Poe[28], Hugging Face’s HuggingChat[9], ByteDance’s Coze[3], and Ten-
cent’s Yuanqi[37], which simplify AI adoption by providing users with pre-configured, customizable
applications [42]. The second paradigm involves LLM agents developed using frameworks like
LangChain [16], AutoGPT [11], Langroid [18], AutoGen [23], and LlamaIndex [22], which offer
greater programmability and modularity, allowing developers to build sophisticated, multi-agent
systems that integrate external tools and dynamic workflows [20]. Despite their advantages, both
paradigms remain architecturally fragmented and lack standardized interoperability, leading to
redundant development efforts and constrained scalability.
From a software engineering (SE) perspective, current LLM application paradigms resemble
traditional platform-centric software ecosystems, where applications are tightly coupled to
proprietary APIs and execution environments. LLM app stores, while lowering the barrier to entry,
impose constraints on extensibility and cross-platform interoperability , leading to vendor
lock-in and duplicated development efforts across different ecosystems. In contrast, agent-based
LLM frameworks provide modularity but lack standardized mechanisms for component reuse
and integration, making it challenging to compose LLM applications that seamlessly operate
across heterogeneous environments. This fragmentation mirrors historical challenges in SE, where
monolithic architectures have given way to service-oriented and microservices-based designs
to improve reusability, scalability, and maintainability. Another key limitation of existing LLM
applications is inefficient hardware utilization. Most rely on cloud-based execution, which, despite
offering scalable computation, introduces latency, operational costs, and dependence on centralized

To address these challenges, we propose a new paradigm for LLM applications based on open

• A Software-Defined Approach to LLM Applications: We propose a structured design

2 FUTURE VISION FOR LLM APPLICATIONS
This section explores emerging trends in LLM applications, highlighting their potential to create a
more interconnected and intelligent AI landscape.
2.1 Open Ecosystems
LLM app stores, such as GPT Store[25], FowGPT [38], and Yuanqi[37], have emerged as platforms
for distributing and customizing AI-driven applications. They offer features like instructions,
knowledge bases, and plugin integration, making AI adoption more accessible. However, these
platforms are mostly centralized and proprietary, limiting cross-platform interoperability and
collaboration. Beyond app stores, LLM agents provide a more autonomous approach, enabling task
automation and multi-agent collaboration. Frameworks like LangChain [16], AutoGPT [11], and
LlamaIndex [22] allow agents to plan, reason, and execute tasks independently. However, current
implementations remain fragmented, lacking standardized communication protocols for secure and
scalable cooperation. Recent efforts such asModel Context Protocol (MCP) [1], Agent Network Protocol
(ANP) [6], and Internet of Agents (IoA) [7] have emerged. MCP, proposed by Anthropic, aims to
integrate LLMs with external tools and resources by treating the entire internet as a model’s context,
enabling more seamless and dynamic interactions. ANP focuses on building a decentralized, secure,
and scalable communication framework for AI agents, incorporating identity management and
encrypted communication to ensure trust and security in multi-agent collaboration. IoA, inspired
by the traditional internet architecture, envisions a global network where agents can autonomously
register, discover, and communicate, fostering an open environment for large-scale AI cooperation.
While these protocols represent important steps toward an open ecosystem , they remain
in the early stages of development, and a comprehensive, structured approach is still needed to
achieve widespread adoption.
The limitations of LLM applications underscore the need for a more structured and interoperable
architecture. Existing platforms offer glimpses of what is possible but remain constrained by
fragmentation and lack of standardization. To realize the full potential of LLM applications, there
is a pressing need for an open ecosystem that enables seamless interaction across diverse
LLM applications, platforms, and services . Addressing these challenges requires a shift toward
a more modular, decoupled design that ensures flexibility, security, and extensibility.
2.2 Hardware Synergy
While LLM applications have traditionally been software-driven, AI hardware is now transforming
how users engage with intelligent systems. Devices like AI phones, smart earbuds, and aug-
mented reality glasses are moving AI interactions from cloud-dependent models to real-time,
edge-computing environments. Innovative products such as Ola Friend [4] provide emotional com-
panionship through natural voice interactions, while AI Pin [13] allows users to create personalized
AI assistants with full data control. Friend [33] offers a wearable AI chat companion with built-in
privacy protection, and Plaud Note [26] and Plaud NotePin [27] enhance productivity by transcribing
and summarizing conversations using advanced LLMs. These developments illustrate the growing
synergy between LLMs and dedicated hardware , making intelligent systems more accessible
and seamlessly integrated into daily life.
Despite these advancements, many AI wearables and devices remain constrained within propri-
etary ecosystems, limiting their ability to connect with third-party LLM applications or agent-based
systems. Cloud-based processing, while powerful, also presents challenges related to latency and pri-
vacy. For AI to fully integrate into everyday experiences, hardware and software must be designed
with interoperability in mind. A more flexible architecture that connects LLM applications,

3 THREE-LAYER DECOUPLED ARCHITECTURE
The three-layer decoupled architecture is shown in the Figure 1. The Application Layer focuses
on LLM configuration, multimodal interactions, and distribution channels. The Protocol Layer
manages session authentication, task orchestration, and communication protocols. The Hardware
Layer enables LLM applications to be deployed on various computing devices. This decoupled
design ensures flexibility, scalability, and cross-platform adaptability for LLM applications.
3.1 Application Layer
The Application Layer serves as the interface between end users, developers, and LLM ap-
plications, enabling the design, configuration, interaction, and distribution of AI services .
This layer abstracts the complexities of the underlying Protocol Layer and Hardware Layer, allowing
seamless deployment across different platforms while ensuring flexibility and adaptability.
At the core of this layer is LLM app configuration , where developers and managers can
define the behavior and capabilities of their applications. This includes setting up persona and
prompt resources, selecting a base model, configuring input/output formats, and adjusting generation
diversity. Additionally, applications can integrate knowledge bases, allowing them to retrieve
and recall information from structured and unstructured data sources such as local documents,
online content, and third-party APIs. To enhance functionality, LLM applications can incorporate
built-in skills (e.g., web search, image generation, code interpretation) or extend their capabilities
through external plugins and APIs . These features enable applications to perform specialized
tasks, interact with external services, and execute dynamic workflows. The workflow system
further allows developers to define multi-step processes, facilitating complex decision-making and
task automation. Once configured, applications can be published and distributed through official
LLM app stores, third-party marketplaces, or enterprise channels , making them accessible to users
across various platforms. The multi-modal interaction support ensures users can engage with
applications through text, voice, image, video, and code-based inputs, broadening their usability
across different domains. When LLM applications are deployed on hardware devices, even more
input modalities become available, such as sensor data, gestures, eye tracking, and biometric signals.
Existing LLM applications often suffer from limited scalability, inefficient execution, and
poor cross-platform adaptability due to their monolithic design, where authentication, task
management, and communication are tightly integrated within the application itself. In our proposed
Three-Layer Decoupled Architecture, the Application Layer offloads these functions to the
Protocol Layer, enabling more efficient session management, dynamic task orchestration,
and optimized real-time communication . This separation allows LLM applications to be more
flexible, scalable, and interoperable across different platforms and computing environments.
3.2 Protocol Layer
The Protocol Layer plays a dual role: breaking down barriers between different platforms
while also providing a secure bridge to hardware components . By establishing a structured
communication framework, the Protocol Layer enables LLM applications to interact fluidly across
platforms and hardware environments, unlocking their full potential in real-world applications.
3.2.1 Session Management. Traditional OAuth-based identity systems [2] are often centralized and
not optimized for AI-native workflows, leading to platform lock-in. To address this, the Protocol

techniques (e.g., TLS session tickets [ 35], QUIC [ 17]) to maintain secure connections without
repeated handshakes. Additionally, it supports multiplexed connections, optimizing bandwidth
usage and enabling multiple AI workflows to run concurrently over a single secure channel.
3.2.2 Task Orchestration. Task allocation efficiently distributes computational workloads across
available resources. Inspired by distributed computing frameworks like Kubernetes [5] and Ray [24],
the Protocol Layer employs intelligent scheduling based on latency, computational cost, and real-
time system load, ensuring balanced workloads across cloud, edge, and specialized accelerators.
Model invocation enables seamless interaction with external models and services. Using standard-
ized model-serving protocols, LLM applications can dynamically switch between local execution
and remote model calls, optimizing efficiency while selecting the most suitable model for each task.
Edge computing optimization reduces latency and cloud dependence by processing tasks closer
to the data source. The Protocol Layer supports adaptive inference, enabling models to run on edge
devices when network conditions or privacy constraints limit cloud execution. Techniques such as
model quantization, pruning, and federated learning further improve performance and resource
efficiency, ensuring smooth operation even in bandwidth-limited or offline scenarios.
3.2.3 Transport Protocols. Low-latency communication is crucial for maintaining responsive-
ness in AI-driven applications, such as conversational AI, autonomous systems, and real-time
analytics. The Protocol Layer leverages transport protocols like QUIC [ 17], which reduces con-
nection setup time through 0-RTT handshakes, and gRPC [ 40], which enables efficient binary
serialization using Protocol Buffers. These optimizations minimize latency and improve throughput,
ensuring that AI-generated responses are delivered instantly, even in high-concurrency environ-
ments. Streaming data transmission allows LLM applications to handle continuous data flows,
enabling real-time interaction with external models and hardware devices. Unlike traditional request-
response models, streaming protocols such as WebSockets [10], HTTP/2 Server Push [30, 43], and
MQTT [21] support persistent connections, reducing overhead from repeated handshakes. This is
beneficial for applications like live inference, sensor data aggregation, and AI-powered augmented
reality systems, where uninterrupted, low-latency data exchange is required.
3.2.4 Network Communication. AI device networking enables seamless communication between
LLM applications across cloud servers, edge devices, and embedded AI systems. By using stan-
dardized protocols, LLM applications efficiently exchange data in heterogeneous environments,
enabling real-time collaboration between AI-powered devices such as smart assistants, autonomous
robots, and IoT-driven industrial systems for synchronized, context-aware interactions. Remote
LLM invocation allows applications to access external models and computational resources based
on workload demands. Using Federated Model Invocation (FMI) and secure API gateways, LLM ap-
plications can invoke high-capacity remote models when local resources are insufficient. Adaptive
model routing and load-aware inference delegation direct requests to the most suitable LLM

3.3 Hardware Layer
This layer underpins AI execution with specialized processors, communication modules, and
security frameworks. Integrated with the Protocol Layer, it supports dynamic workload allocation,
secure communication, and optimized inference across cloud, edge, and embedded systems.
3.3.1 Input Devices. Input devices like microphones, cameras, and biometric sensors provide es-
sential data for LLM applications, enabling real-time speech recognition, image processing, and
multi-modal interactions. To ensure privacy, the Hardware Layer incorporates on-device encryp-
tion, secure data pipelines, and privacy-preserving inference techniques that minimize raw
data exposure. By processing sensitive data locally whenever possible, devices can reduce reliance
on cloud-based processing, enhancing user privacy and responsiveness.
3.3.2 Processing Units. AI accelerators, embedded processors, and edge AI chips form the computa-
tional backbone of LLM applications. AI accelerators such as NPUs, TPUs, and GPUs are optimized
for high-throughput model execution, delivering efficient inference for complex AI tasks. Embedded
processors power low-energy AI applications in wearables and IoT devices, while edge AI chips
enable local execution of LLMs, reducing latency and dependency on external networks. To enhance
security, AI sandboxing techniques isolate execution environments, preventing unauthorized data
access and ensuring controlled AI interactions. Additionally, hardware-backed trusted execution
environments (TEEs) [19, 31] like Intel SGX and ARM TrustZone provide secure enclaves for
sensitive computations, ensuring model integrity and confidential processing.
3.3.3 Communication Modules. AI-native networking protocols, including Bluetooth, WiFi,
UWB, and 5G, enable low-latency synchronization, ensuring smooth communication across diverse
hardware environments [32]. To optimize workload distribution, AI task routing mechanisms
dynamically allocate computational tasks between cloud, edge, and local infrastructures, enhancing
efficiency and responsiveness. Security is reinforced through end-to-end encryption mechanisms
like TLS 1.3 [8] and QUIC-based transport security [14], while zero-trust authentication frameworks
prevent unauthorized access to AI-driven services. Additionally, Differential privacy safeguards
transmitted data, balancing security with AI interaction accuracy.
3.3.4 Embedded AI Systems. The integration of AI into embedded systems such as AI glasses,
smartwatches, AI earbuds, and IoT-driven automation platforms is redefining the role of LLM
applications in real-world environments. These systems leverage hybrid AI execution strategies,
seamlessly switching between local processing and cloud-based inference based on latency re-
quirements, energy efficiency, and computational demands. The Protocol Layer facilitates this
adaptability by enabling intelligent model selection, allowing devices to determine whether to
execute tasks locally or offload them to remote LLM services. This dynamic allocation ensures
optimal performance while maintaining a balance between privacy, efficiency, and responsiveness.
3.3.5 Security and privacy infrastructure. Ensuring the security and privacy of AI-powered devices
is fundamental to the Hardware Layer, enabling trustworthy and resilient AI interactions. Privacy
control mechanisms such as encrypted model storage, federated learning for decentralized AI train-
ing, and policy-driven access control safeguard user data while allowing AI models to improve over
time without exposing sensitive information. To mitigate security risks, AI-driven threat detection
systems employ real-time anomaly detection to identify adversarial attacks or unauthorized access,
ensuring the integrity of AI interactions. Furthermore, secure firmware updates and model integrity

4 CHALLENGES & OPPORTUNITIES
4.1 Challenges
4.1.1 Challenges in Secure Plugin Execution for LLM Applications. The Application Layer extends
LLM functionality via external plugins, but securely isolating them remains challenging. Traditional
sandboxing introduces performance overhead, while dynamic execution increases risks of side-
channel attacks and dependency vulnerabilities. Unlike static components, LLM plugins are
installed and updated dynamically, complicating authentication and integrity verification .
Ensuring only trusted plugins are loaded requires strong cryptographic checks, but excessive
security can hinder usability. Additionally, plugins interacting with external APIs posedata leakage
risks, making strict access control difficult without sacrificing functionality.
4.1.2 Challenges in Privacy-Preserving Task Orchestration. The Protocol Layer enables LLMs to
offload tasks across cloud, edge, and embedded systems, but transmitting intermediate computations
increases risks of data leakage, model inversion attacks, and unauthorized access . While
homomorphic encryption and secure multi-party computation (SMPC) offer privacy, they
are often too slow for real-time applications. LLMs must also comply with data privacy regu-
lations like GDPR and CCPA, which mandate transparency and user control over personal data.
Ensuring on-demand data deletion is particularly challenging, as current model unlearning
techniques struggle to efficiently remove specific user data without full retraining, leading to
high computational overhead. Additionally, balancing privacy with model performance remains a
key challenge—overly aggressive anonymization or differential privacy techniques can degrade AI
accuracy, reducing the effectiveness of personalized interactions and adaptive learning capabilities.
4.1.3 Challenges in Testing and Debugging the Architectures. The three-layer decoupled archi-
tecture enhances scalability but complicates end-to-end testing and debugging . LLMs are
non-deterministic, making error reproduction difficult, while security threats like prompt injec-
tion, adversarial perturbations, and model inversion attacks span multiple layers. Traditional
security testing methods, such asfuzz testing and static analysis, are poorly suited for evaluating
AI-specific threats, as they fail to capture the dynamic and probabilistic nature of LLM responses.
Moreover, automated testing frameworks struggle to assess LLM robustness, bias, and fairness ,
further complicating the validation of LLM applications.
4.1.4 Challenges in Secure AI Hardware Deployment. The Hardware Layer enables LLM execution
on AI accelerators and edge devices, but preventingmodel extraction, modification, and misuse
is a major challenge.Model extraction attacks threaten deployed models, and existing protections
like model watermarking and encrypted storage are not foolproof. AI-powered devices, such as
smart glasses and wearable, require low-latency inference, yet securing them against tampering,
adversarial attacks, and unauthorized access often adds computational overhead. Additionally,
ensuring secure firmware updates, TEE, and supply chain integrity is increasingly difficult,
as attackers may exploit hardware backdoors or inject malicious firmware. Balancing security with
efficiency remains a persistent challenge.
4.2 Opportunities
4.2.1 Secure and Trustworthy LLM Plugin Frameworks. Future research can focus on designing
granular and adaptive security models for LLM plugins, ensuring fine-grained access control

without restricting functionality. This includes exploringcontext-aware permission frameworks
that dynamically adjust plugin privileges based on factors such as user intent, execution context,
and risk assessment. Furthermore, advancements in TEEs and hardware-based attestation offer
opportunities to develop secure LLM plugin ecosystems. Investigating how TEEs can be integrated
with AI-driven systems may enable verifiable execution of untrusted plugins while maintaining
data confidentiality. Additionally, studying lightweight and efficient sandboxing techniques ,
specifically optimized for machine learning workloads, can help isolate plugins without imposing
significant performance overhead.
4.2.2 Privacy-Preserving Task Execution. With increasing concerns about data privacy, research can
explore federated inference strategies that allow LLMs to process tasks locally while aggregating
encrypted updates across distributed environments. Unlike traditional cloud-based inference, this
approach minimizes raw data exposure while maintaining model performance. Another promising
direction is developing adaptive privacy mechanisms that balance security with computational
efficiency. This includes exploring differentially private inference techniques that dynamically
adjust privacy levels based on task sensitivity and user preferences. Additionally, research into
efficient model unlearning algorithms can address the challenge of removing specific user
interactions from LLMs without requiring costly full retraining. Techniques such as incremental
model updates, selective forgetting, and reversible AI workflows may provide a more scalable
approach to compliance with data privacy regulations.
4.2.3 Automated Testing and Security Validation for LLM Architectures. Ensuring LLM reliability
requires advanced automated testing methodologies . Research can focus on self-learning
testing frameworks using reinforcement learning to generate diverse test cases. Adversarial
security validation is another key area, where automated tools probe LLMs for vulnerabilities via
synthetic adversarial prompts, testing resilience against prompt injection, model inversion, and
data leakage. Additionally, real-time anomaly detection can help identify unexpected behaviors
and mitigate risks. Integrating formal verification techniques into LLM security assessments can
further enhance trust, providing mathematical guarantees of correctness for critical applications in
healthcare, finance, and autonomous systems.
4.2.4 Secure AI Hardware for Next-Generation LLM Applications. Research onhardware-accelerated
encryption can enable privacy-preserving inference with minimal overhead. Developingconfiden-
tial AI computing frameworks using TEEs and secure enclaves can enhance model protection
while ensuring efficient large-scale deployment. Exploring secure AI chip architectures with
built-in defenses against adversarial attacks, model extraction, and tampering can further strengthen
security. These defenses include memory protection, secure boot, and tamper-resistant accelerators.
Ensuring supply chain integrity is another key challenge. Investigating hardware provenance
verification and blockchain-based trust mechanisms can help prevent compromised AI models
and safeguard LLM execution environments.
5 CONCLUSION
The future of LLM applications depends on overcoming platform silos, fragmented hardware inte-
gration, and interoperability challenges. This paper envisions a three-layer decoupled architecture
to enhance modularity, efficiency, and cross-platform compatibility. Addressing key security and
privacy challenges is crucial for safe and scalable AI deployment. We also highlight open research
directions in software architecture and security engineering to support the next generation of
AI systems. An open, secure, and adaptable AI infrastructure will be essential for driving future
advancements in LLM application ecosystems.
